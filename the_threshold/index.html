Where the threshold sits

One grid is a coin toss near the critical point. Repeat it many times for each p and the chance of a spanning path turns into an S-curve. The bigger the grid, the steeper the S, closing in on a step at pc ≈ 0.5927, the site-percolation threshold of the infinite square lattice.

How the flood works

Every open cell in the top row is a source and enters the queue at layer 0. Each step takes the whole current layer, wets every open, still-dry neighbour, and makes those the next layer. The flood stops when a layer adds nothing, so every cell reachable from the top ends up wet.

The grid percolates if any bottom-row cell is wet. Because breadth-first search reaches cells in order of distance, the first bottom cell to get wet ends the shortest open route from the top; following each cell's parent back up draws it in claret.

The flip side: rot

Turn the question round. Start with a sound structure, every cell intact, and let cells rot one at a time in a random order. The top keeps its link to the bottom for a long while, then one more rotten cell cuts it. On a large grid that happens when about 40.7% of the cells have gone.

Why 40%: the dual threshold

Intact cells pass the flood through their four side neighbours. Look at the same grid from the rot's side and the condition turns over: the top is cut off from the bottom exactly when a cluster of rotten cells runs from the left edge to the right edge. Rot needs only a corner to block, since water cannot squeeze diagonally between two rotten cells, so rot clusters join eight ways. It is the board game Hex in another shape: one side's path is the other side's wall.

So the two questions share one boundary. Four-neighbour percolation of intact cells and eight-neighbour percolation of rot are a matching pair of lattices, and their thresholds add up to one: pc + pc* = 1. With pc ≈ 0.592746 for the intact cells, rot spans at pc* ≈ 0.407254. Exhibit 4 is Exhibit 2 read from the other side: each run's break fraction is the spot where that run's own S-curve would jump, and as N grows those spots crowd in on the dashed line.

Random rot and an attacker are different stories. Rot falling at random has to claim about 41% of the grid before a wall forms by chance. An attacker who chooses the cells needs one per column, N cells, 1/N of the grid: 3.3% at N = 30, under 1% at N = 120. A percolation threshold measures robustness to random damage, not to aimed damage.

Connectivity is not strength. These exhibits ask only whether some path survives. Just before the break, that path is a thin, stringy cluster hanging on single cells; the current it could carry falls to zero at the threshold (in two dimensions roughly as (p − pc)1.3) and its stiffness falls faster still. A real structure carries load, and each failure shifts load onto its neighbours, so failures cascade and it can give way well before its last path is cut. Read 40% as an upper bound on the random rot a structure survives, not as a safety margin.

Original simulation; nothing is fetched. Grids come from a seeded generator (mulberry32), so a seed reproduces the grid. Each cell draws one uniform number u and is open when u < p. The threshold pc ≈ 0.592746 is the published estimate for site percolation on the square lattice. Rot mode shuffles the cells with the same generator; its histogram follows Newman and Ziff, adding rotten cells in random order and joining eight-way neighbours with union-find until one cluster touches both sides.
