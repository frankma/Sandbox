Guess the other coin: one says “same”, the other says “opposite”, and they never lose

Two players each toss a fair coin, separately. Each sees only their own coin, then guesses the other's. They win together if at least one guess is right and lose if both are wrong. They can agree a plan beforehand but cannot talk after the toss. Is there a plan that wins every time?

Every plan

A player's guess can only depend on their own coin, so each has just four plans: say the same, say the opposite, always say heads or always say tails. A random plan is a mix of these four, so it can't beat the best of them. Of the 16 pairs, two win every toss.

Why it works

Count the right guesses.

Each guess is right exactly half the time, whatever the plan. A is guessing B's coin, which A never sees and which doesn't depend on A's coin. So across the four tosses, any plan of A's is right in exactly two, and the same goes for B. That makes four right guesses in all, whatever the players agree.

Winning every toss needs exactly one right guess per toss. There are four tosses and only four right guesses to share out. Every toss needs at least one, so no toss can have two: the guesses must never be right at the same time.

“Same” and “opposite” split the tosses perfectly. “Same” is right on HH and TT; “opposite” is right on HT and TH. Every toss is covered once. When both players say “same”, they are right together on HH and TT and wrong together on HT and TH, which wastes two right guesses and wins only half the time.

The quant lesson is that you can't make either bet better than 50%, but you can choose how the two bets are correlated. The winning plan makes A's and B's hits perfectly negatively correlated, the way a hedge pairs a loss on one leg with a gain on the other.
